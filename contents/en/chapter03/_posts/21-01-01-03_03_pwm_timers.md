## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can state what a duty cycle does to the average voltage of a resistive load, and you can say what it only *approximately* does to a small DC motor. You will generate a visible LED breathe on a safe GPIO using `ledcAttach` and `ledcWrite` in ESP32 Arduino 3.x, recognize why a bare `analogWrite` is not a promise of 8-bit 0–255 on that core, and set the same idea in MicroPython with `PWM` and `duty_u16`. You will also explain why a 20% duty on a 7.4 V motor rail is often below the start of a TT gearmotor, and why this lab still refuses to attach that motor.

## Prerequisites

You can blink a pin from the [Chapter 02 serial heartbeat]({% multilang_post_url contents/chapter02/02_04_blink_serial_hello %}) and you can run the non-blocking loop from Lesson 1, so a brightness ramp does not become `delay` calls that last a whole fade. ## Why it matters

Capstone A's differential-drive teleop does not send a torque. It sends two duties, one toward PWMA and one toward PWMB on a TB6612, and the driver switches the motor supply at that pattern. When commands go silent for 300 ms, both duties become 0, which is how the wheels are told to stop commanding torque. Practicing that number on an LED means you can see 0%, 25%, 50%, and 90% before a gearbox is involved. A motor on a GPIO would skip the driver and dump coil current into a pin that was designed for an LED. Wheels stay up even later, when the driver is finally connected, until a low duty has been watched with the chassis in the air.

## Core teaching

A timer toggles a pin high for part of a fixed period and low for the rest. The duty cycle $D$ is the fraction of the period spent high, so $0 \le D \le 1$. For a resistive load on a stiff supply the average voltage is close to

$$
V_{\mathrm{avg}} \approx D \cdot V_{\mathrm{source}}.
$$

An LED plus its series resistor is close enough to that picture that brightness tracks $D$, as long as $D \cdot V_{\mathrm{source}}$ still clears the LED's forward drop for part of the cycle. The eye integrates the pulses. You do not need the LED to switch at a rate you can count.

![Duty at 25%, 50%, and 90%]({{ site.imgurl }}/generated/pwm_duty_cycle.png)

A DC motor is not a resistor. The winding has resistance and inductance, and the rotor produces a back voltage once it is spinning. Duty still sets the average voltage the winding sees, and speed follows that average only roughly, with a dead zone at low duty where friction and the brush drop eat the whole average. Raising $D$ from 0.05 to 0.15 may do nothing visible. Raising it through the band where the motor actually starts produces a jump, not a straight line. Frequency matters as much as duty. Around 100 Hz many small gearmotors chatter and whine: the torque pulses once per period and the gearbox speaks. LED PWM can sit near 1 kHz, which is already hard to see as flicker. Motor PWM is usually placed between 1 kHz and 20 kHz so the current ripple is smaller and the whine moves up, sometimes above hearing. Twenty kilohertz is a normal choice for the TB6612 inputs later. One kilohertz is the right choice for this LED, because you are debugging duty, not inductance.

On a classic AVR Arduino, `analogWrite(pin, 128)` means "about half of an 8-bit range," and the range really is 0 to 255. That sentence does not travel to the ESP32 Arduino core. In ESP32 Arduino 3.x the supported API is the LEDC peripheral: attach a pin with a frequency and a resolution, then write a duty that fits that resolution. An 8-bit resolution still gives the familiar 0 to 255, but only because you asked for 8 bits. A 10-bit attach makes 255 a quarter-scale command. Read the call. Do not copy an AVR snippet and assume the wheel will creep.

```cpp
const int PWM_PIN = 18;       // safe on a typical ESP32 DevKit; not a strap pin
const uint32_t PWM_HZ = 1000; // LED lab. A later motor channel wants 20000.
const uint8_t PWM_BITS = 8;   // duty 0..255 only because resolution is 8

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  ledcAttach(PWM_PIN, PWM_HZ, PWM_BITS);
}

void loop() {
  static uint32_t last = 0;
  static int duty = 0;
  static int step = 5;
  uint32_t now = millis();
  if (now - last >= 15) {
    last = now;
    duty += step;
    if (duty >= 255 || duty <= 0) {
      step = -step;
      duty = constrain(duty, 0, 255);
    }
    ledcWrite(PWM_PIN, duty);
    Serial.printf("duty=%d frac=%.2f\n", duty, duty / 255.0);
  }
}
```

The 15 ms step is a brightness update, not a nap that replaces the loop. There is no `delay(15)`. When this breathe later shares a loop with a 300 ms timeout, the same `millis()` test is what keeps the timeout awake. For awareness, the AVR-style call looks like `analogWrite(9, 128)`. On ESP32 Arduino 3.x, prefer `ledcAttach` and `ledcWrite`. If a core happens to provide `analogWrite` as a wrapper, the resolution and default frequency are still whatever that wrapper chose, so the explicit LEDC calls stay in your file.

MicroPython exposes the same idea with a 16-bit duty even when you are thinking in percent:

```python
from machine import Pin, PWM
import time

pwm = PWM(Pin(18), freq=1000, duty_u16=0)  # LED. Use freq=20000 for a motor input later.
duty = 0
step = 1200
last = time.ticks_ms()

while True:
    now = time.ticks_ms()
    if time.ticks_diff(now, last) >= 15:
        last = now
        duty += step
        if duty >= 65535 or duty <= 0:
            step = -step
            duty = max(0, min(65535, duty))
        pwm.duty_u16(duty)
        print("duty_u16", duty)
    time.sleep_ms(1)
```

`duty_u16` runs from 0 to 65535. A fraction $D$ is `int(D * 65535)`. Frequency is the `freq` argument in hertz. Changing `freq` to 20000 does not make the LED a motor, and it may make a cheap LED driver ring; keep 1000 Hz until the load is the TB6612 input.

ESP32 strapping pins are sampled at reset to decide boot mode and flash voltage. The ones students hit with a PWM wire are GPIO 0, GPIO 2, GPIO 12, and GPIO 15. A pulldown or a peripheral on those pins can stop the chip from booting, or can make the next upload fail in a way that looks like a bad cable. GPIO 2 is also a common onboard LED, which is why Chapter 02 blinked it and why this lesson does not also use it as a motor-shaped experiment. Pick GPIO 18 or GPIO 19 for PWM labs on a DevKit module. Confirm the silk. If a pin is tied to flash or to USB-serial on your particular board, believe the schematic over this paragraph.

The TB6612, when you buy it, takes a logic-level PWM on its input pins and switches a separate motor supply VM. VM wants about 4.5 V to 10 V, and the chip's continuous current rating on the usual module is about 1.2 A. Those numbers are why a 7.4 V battery belongs on VM later and never on a GPIO. This chapter does not wire that module. The link is for the week you are actually ready to leave the LED: the module at [hshop, TB6612FNG](https://hshop.vn/mach-dieu-khien-dong-co-dc-tb6612fng-dc-motor-driver) is listed at 45000₫. Until the state machine in Lesson 5 can force duty 0, VM stays disconnected and the wheels stay up.

## Worked example

A two-cell pack sits near 7.4 V. A duty of 20% would present an average near

$$
V_{\mathrm{avg}} \approx 0.20 \times 7.4 = 1.48\,\mathrm{V},
$$

about 1.5 V. A yellow TT gearmotor often does not start there. The brushes and the gearbox want something closer to a few volts before the shaft moves, so the first motion test you will run in a later chapter starts nearer 30% to 40% with the wheels up:

$$
0.30 \times 7.4 \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{V}, \qquad 0.40 \times 7.4 \approx 3.0\,\mathrm{V}.
$$

Those are still averages, not a promise of speed, and they assume the driver is in the circuit. If you skip the driver and put 7.4 V on a GPIO, you are no longer in the dead-zone discussion. You are past the absolute maximum of the pin.

On the LED bench the same 20% is an 8-bit duty of $0.20 \times 255 \approx 51$, or a MicroPython value of $0.20 \times 65535 \approx 13107$. Fifty percent is 128 in 8 bits and 32768 in `duty_u16`. Ninety percent is about 230, or about 58982. Log those three numbers once so the checklist has a table, not a vibe. The figure at the top of the lesson is those three pulses drawn on one period: a short high, a square half, and a high that only dips briefly.

A 100 Hz motor PWM has period $1/100 = 10$ ms. The torque dips every 10 ms, which is audible and visible as chatter. Moving to 20 kHz shortens the period to $50$ µs. The winding inductance averages the current, and the duty you computed above is what remains. The LED lab does not need that rate. Do not "upgrade" the breathe to 20 kHz and then conclude PWM is broken because your eye cannot see a difference between frequencies that are both flicker-free.

## Hands-on lab

The load is the LED and 180 Ω resistor from the GPIO lesson, on GPIO 18, unless your silk says that pin is taken. USB power only.

1. Attach PWM at 1 kHz, 8-bit, and run the breathe. You should see a smooth brighten and dim, and the serial log should climb and fall rather than stick at one number. Photograph the LED at a dim step and a bright step, or paste three log lines whose duties match about 25%, 50%, and 90%.
2. Hold the duty at 0 and confirm the LED is dark. Hold it at 255, or `duty_u16` at 65535, and confirm it matches a plain `digitalWrite(HIGH)` in brightness. Those two endpoints are what "PWM is zero" and "PWM is full" mean before a driver exists.
3. Move the frequency to 100 Hz for a few seconds. On an LED you may see flicker. That flicker is the motor-chatter lesson without a motor. Put the frequency back to 1 kHz.
4. Do not clip the LED leads onto a battery, and do not jumper GPIO 18 to a motor terminal "just to see." If the chip fails to reboot after you experiment, check whether the wire is on GPIO 0, 2, 12, or 15, then move it.

## Exercises

1. A 7.4 V pack and a duty of 0.35 are the first spin you plan with wheels up. Compute the average voltage. Convert 0.35 into an 8-bit `ledcWrite` argument and into `duty_u16`.
2. Someone calls `ledcAttach(18, 1000, 10)` and then `ledcWrite(18, 255)`, expecting half brightness. What fraction did they actually command, and what count is true half-scale at 10 bits?
3. Why is 100 Hz a poor motor PWM frequency, and why is the same 100 Hz tolerable as a short LED experiment in step 3 of the lab?
4. Name two ESP32 pins you will not use for this breathe, and the pin this lesson does use. What boot-time decision are the avoided pins involved in?
