A hobby servo hides a small DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer, and a control loop inside one plastic case. You command it with a pulse width, not with the duty-cycle PWM you just used on a wheel. This lesson separates those two signals, treats the kg·cm number on the packet as a stall figure, and shows you how to sweep a horn without cooking the motor against a hard stop.

## Learning objectives

1. State the 50 Hz frame and the meaning of about 1.0 ms, 1.5 ms, and 2.0 ms pulses, including that some servos travel over 0.5–2.5 ms.
2. Compute the 7.5% duty of a 1.5 ms pulse in a 20 ms frame, and explain why a motor-style `analogWrite` produces a different signal.
3. Read a kg·cm torque rating as a stall figure at a stated voltage, and distinguish a positional servo from a continuous-rotation unit.
4. Power the servo from a 5 V rail that can supply stall current, share ground with the microcontroller, and stop a sweep that meets a mechanical stop.

## Prerequisites

You can measure 5 V with a meter and you know the H-bridge PWM from lesson 05-01 well enough to notice that this pulse is a different shape. You can use a servo library, or you can generate a microsecond pulse on your board. A paper protractor, tape, and a 5 V supply that can deliver about an ampere are enough hardware. If you have no servo, the calculation half of the lab still stands.

## Why this matters for Capstone A and the ROS path

Capstone A's drive does not need a servo. Two brushed motors and an H-bridge do the rolling. A small pan servo is optional later, for a distance sensor or a camera that must look left and right. When you eventually describe that joint to ROS, the controller speaks in position or trajectory of a joint, and a driver node turns that position into the hobby pulse. The pulse itself is a firmware detail. Getting the pulse, the supply, and the horn spline right now means a later joint does not jitter, brown out the robot, or strip a plastic horn on the first demo. The wheel PWM you sign in Chapter 07 stays a wheel PWM. You will not "servo-write" a drive motor.

## The pulse inside a 20 ms frame

Hobby servos expect a frame about every 20 ms, which is 50 Hz. Inside that frame the high time is the command. About 1.0 ms sits near one end of travel, 1.5 ms is the mechanical center on a typical positional servo, and 2.0 ms sits near the other end. Some servos, and some extended-range settings, travel from about 0.5 ms to 2.5 ms. The diagram in this lesson draws that high time inside the longer frame. The library call you want is a microsecond write: 1000, 1500, and 2000.

The duty-cycle trap is numerical. A 1.5 ms pulse divided by a 20 ms frame is 7.5 percent. `analogWrite` on a typical motor pin emits a much faster PWM whose duty you set as a fraction of a short period. A 7.5 percent duty at 1 kHz is a high time of 75 µs, which a servo does not read as "center." Use the servo library for your board, or a timer that really holds the line high for the microsecond count you asked for, and repeats that pulse about every 20 ms.

## Torque, gears, and the servo that is actually a motor

The number printed in kg·cm is a stall torque at a stated voltage, often 4.8 V or 6 V. It is the twist the horn can hold when the shaft is already stopped and the motor is pulling full current. It is not a license to stall the servo as a clamp. An SG90 is a small 9 g positional servo: useful for a light sensor pan, easy to strip if the horn hits a bracket and you keep commanding the far pulse. A metal-gear MG90S is the usual substitute when the plastic gears skip. It still stalls and still heats; the gears survive longer.

A continuous-rotation servo is a different part that uses the same three-wire plug. Its potentiometer is replaced by a trim, and the pulse commands speed and direction, not an angle. If your horn spins freely for many turns at 1000 µs and sits still near 1500 µs, you have a continuous unit. Park it for a wheel experiment someday. It will not hold a camera angle.

## Power, ground, and heat

Feed the red wire from a 5 V rail that can supply the stall current. For an SG90 that is on the order of 0.5–0.7 A at stall, more than the 3.3 V pin of an ESP32 can provide, and more than a USB port enjoys if the horn is loaded. The signal wire may be driven at 3.3 V; most hobby servos read that as a valid high. The black or brown wire shares ground with the microcontroller and with the 5 V supply. If that ground is missing, the pulses are measured against the wrong reference and the horn twitches or walks to a stop.

A stalled servo gets hot in seconds because the motor inside is a small DC motor at nearly stall current, and the loop keeps driving it. Your sweep must not command a pulse past a horn that has already hit a screw or the table. If the case becomes hot, command the last free angle, remove power, and move the mechanical stop.

## Worked example

You command 1500 µs, the center pulse.

$$
D = \frac{1.5~\mathrm{ms}}{20~\mathrm{ms}} = 0.075 = 7.5\%
$$

If a pin is instead producing a 1 kHz motor PWM at that same 7.5 percent duty, the high time is

$$
t_{high} = \frac{0.075}{1000~\mathrm{Hz}} = 75~\mu\mathrm{s}
$$

Seventy-five microseconds is not a center command. The servo library's `writeMicroseconds(1500)` is the call that matches the diagram.

Now suppose the code really is sending 1500 µs and the arm still sits about 40 degrees off the angle you wanted. The electronics are doing what you asked. The horn is a press fit on a splined shaft, and it was pushed on at whatever angle it happened to have. Power the servo off, lift the horn, set the servo to 1500 µs briefly to confirm center, power off again, and reseat the horn so the arm matches the protractor's zero. Forcing the horn around with the spline engaged strips the teeth.

## Lab

### Safety

External 5 V only for the servo power pin. Do not use the ESP32 3.3 V pin as the servo supply. Share ground. Keep the horn clear of fingers and of hard stops. If the case heats up, stop the sweep and remove power. One servo at a time.

### BOM

| Item | Role |
|------|------|
| SG90 or MG90S positional servo | The actuator under test |
| 5 V supply capable of about 1 A | Servo power, separate from 3.3 V |
| Microcontroller | Signal pin only |
| Paper protractor and tape | Rough angle log |
| Jumper wires | Signal, 5 V, and common ground |

### Steps

1. Wire 5 V and ground to the servo from the external rail. Wire ground to the microcontroller. Wire the signal pin to a GPIO. Leave power off while you seat the horn loosely.
2. Command 1500 µs. Power on. Note where the horn rests. Power off and reseat the horn so that rest matches a sensible zero on the protractor.
3. Sweep from 1000 µs to 2000 µs in steps of about 50–100 µs, pausing about 200 ms at each step. At three points (1000, 1500, 2000) write the protractor angle.
4. Stop at once if the horn buzzes against a stop or the case becomes hot. Narrow the sweep to the free range.
5. If you have no servo, write the 7.5% calculation, sketch the 20 ms frame with a 1.5 ms pulse, and state the supply rule in your own sentence.

### Expected results

Three angle notes, a statement of the free pulse range you actually used, and a one-line supply note ("5 V external, common ground, signal from GPIO"). A no-hardware path still contains the duty calculation and the sketch.

### Faults

| What you see | What to check |
|--------------|----------------|
| Horn twitches or chatters | Missing common ground, or the pin is still a fast `analogWrite` PWM. |
| Horn drives to a stop and heats | The pulse is outside the mechanical range. Shorten the sweep. |
| ESP32 resets when the servo moves | Servo current is coming from USB or from 3.3 V. Move the red wire to an external 5 V rail. |
| Angle is offset by a fixed amount | Horn spline seating. Power off and reseat. |
| Horn spins continuously | The part is a continuous-rotation servo. Its pulse sets speed, not angle. |

## Exercises

1. What high time, in microseconds, is the usual center, and what duty is that inside a 20 ms frame?
2. A 1 kHz pin runs at 10% duty. What is the high time, and is it inside 1.0–2.0 ms?
3. The packet says 1.8 kg·cm at 4.8 V. What kind of number is that, and what happens electrically at that load?
4. Your 1500 µs command is correct and the arm is 40 degrees off. What do you loosen, and in what power state?
5. Why does Capstone A's drive chassis ignore this lesson's pulse, and where might the pulse return later?

### Answer guidance

Center is 1500 µs, which is 7.5% of 20 ms. A 10% duty at 1 kHz is 100 µs, far below a 1 ms servo pulse, so the servo library is the right tool. The 1.8 kg·cm figure is a stall torque at 4.8 V, and at that load the internal motor is near stall current and heating. Power off, then reseat the horn on the spline. Capstone A drives with DC motors and an H-bridge; a pan servo can return later as a joint whose hobby pulse stays inside firmware.
