A brushed DC motor is the actuator you will bolt onto Capstone A. This lesson explains what the commutator is doing inside the can, why the wire gets hottest when the shaft is stuck, and how four switches let a small logic pin reverse a motor that runs from a battery. You will leave with a truth table for the driver on your own bench, written while the wheels are off the ground.

## Learning objectives

1. Explain how the commutator keeps shaft torque in one direction as the rotor turns, and why armature current is highest at stall.
2. Name the forward switch pair and the reverse switch pair in an H-bridge, and state which switch combinations short the supply.
3. Compute average voltage from an 8-bit PWM duty on a 7.4 V pack, then account for the roughly 1.5–2 V drop of an L298N.
4. Record a one-motor truth table (IN1, IN2, PWM, observed direction) and connect a swapped motor lead to a later wrong-way yaw command.

## Prerequisites

You can measure DC voltage with a multimeter, you can use Ohm's law, and you have already driven a GPIO high and low. From the sensor work, you already know that every module on the robot shares one ground. A battery or a current-limited bench supply is the motor source in this lab. The USB cable stays on the programming port of the microcontroller.

## Why this matters for Capstone A and the ROS path

Chapter 07 signs two PWM commands, one for the left wheel and one for the right wheel. Positive has to mean "this wheel forward" after you have defined forward as the direction of the robot's nose. Much later, a ROS 2 node will receive a `geometry_msgs/Twist`: `linear.x` is the forward speed you asked for, and `angular.z` is the yaw rate. Your firmware, or a small driver node, splits that twist into the two voltages you practice here. A reversed plug on the right motor turns a positive `angular.z` into a yaw the opposite way, and every later controller looks broken while the wiring is simply mirrored. The truth table you write today is the document that keeps that sign honest.

## The commutator keeps the torque facing one way

Inside a brushed DC motor the coils sit on the rotor. Two carbon brushes press on a segmented copper commutator. As the shaft turns, those segments swap which side of each coil touches which brush, so the current in the coil that is sitting under a magnet reverses at the right angle. The torque on the shaft therefore keeps pushing the same way. The photograph in this lesson is that copper barrel and the brushes. When you look at the segments, the reversal is a sliding contact you could point at with a pencil.

The same machine is a generator while it spins. The voltage it produces is the back-EMF, written $$E$$, and it grows with speed. It opposes the supply. The current that remains is

$$
I \approx \frac{V_s - E}{R}
$$

where $$V_s$$ is the voltage actually across the brushes and $$R$$ is the resistance of the winding. At stall the shaft is still, so $$E$$ is about zero and the supply sees only the copper:

$$
I_{stall} \approx \frac{V}{R}
$$

That stall current is the number you hand to the driver choice, the wire gauge, and the fuse. A wheel spinning in the air draws a small no-load current because $$E$$ has risen and cancelled most of $$V_s$$. The next time a gearbox locks against a curb, the motor electrically returns to the stall case even though your PWM command has not changed.

## Four switches, and the states that short the battery

An H-bridge is four switches drawn like the letter H, with the motor as the crossbar. Call them high-side left, low-side left, high-side right, and low-side right. Forward is high-side left on together with low-side right on. Current enters the left brush and returns to ground through the right brush. Reverse swaps the pair: high-side right and low-side left. The current through the copper flips, and the shaft torque flips with it.

Both switches in one vertical leg, high and low on the same side, tie battery positive straight to ground. That fault is called shoot-through. It is a short, and it can destroy the driver in milliseconds. Turning both high sides on is in the same family of mistakes: you have left the single path through the motor, and on a real chip that overlap is a short. You do not invent extra states to "see what happens." Coasting and braking exist, and they are printed in the truth table of the chip you actually bought.

On some drivers, both inputs low leave the winding open and the wheel freewheels. That is coast. On others, the two low sides (or the two inputs held equal) short the winding through the switches so the motor brakes electrically. The L298N, the TB6612, and the DRV8833 do not share one universal sentence here. You read the table for your board, then you confirm it with the shaft and a piece of tape, because a brake you did not ask for will fight the next PWM command and heat the motor while the robot looks stopped.

## PWM on the enable pin

Direction pins choose the path. A PWM signal on the enable, or on a dedicated PWM pin, chops that path on and off. Ignoring the voltage the silicon itself eats,

$$
V_{avg} \approx D \cdot V_s
$$

with duty $$D$$ between 0 and 1. An 8-bit `analogWrite` of 180 means $$D = 180/255$$. The motor inductance smooths the chops into a smaller average voltage, which is why the wheel turns slower as the duty falls, until friction eats the last of the torque and the shaft stops even though the duty is not yet zero.

## Worked example

A 2S pack sits near $$V_s = 7.4~\mathrm{V}$$. You command 180 counts out of 255 on the enable pin.

$$
D = \frac{180}{255} \approx 0.706
$$

$$
V_{avg} \approx 0.706 \times 7.4~\mathrm{V} \approx 5.22~\mathrm{V}
$$

That 5.22 V is the average before the bridge drop. An L298N is a bipolar Darlington bridge and gives up roughly 1.5–2 V while it conducts, so the brushes may see only about 3.2–3.7 V. A TB6612 is a MOSFET bridge and keeps much more of the 5.22 V for the motor. That is why the TB6612 is the preferred driver for Capstone TT gearmotors on 2S. When a robot on an L298N feels weak at "70 percent throttle," compute this number before you blame the gearbox.

## Lab

### Safety

Lift the wheels off the desk, or run a bare motor in a clamp. Motor current comes from a battery or a bench supply with a current limit, never from the USB port that is talking to the microcontroller. Tie the driver ground to the microcontroller ground with a real wire. Keep fingers off the pinion. Start with PWM at 0.

### BOM

| Item | Role |
|------|------|
| ESP32 or Arduino-class board | Two direction pins and one PWM pin |
| One H-bridge module | The board you will name in the next lesson |
| One brushed DC motor (TT gearmotor is ideal) | The shaft you watch |
| 2S pack or a 6–9 V current-limited supply | VM only |
| Multimeter | Confirm VM and the common ground |
| Tape and a notebook | Direction flag and the truth table |

### Steps

1. Photograph the wiring. Measure VM and confirm the microcontroller is still on its own USB supply.
2. Set PWM to 0. Walk IN1 and IN2 through 00, 01, 10, and 11. For each row, record whether the shaft is still, turning one way, turning the other way, or braking (hard to turn by hand) versus coasting (easy to turn).
3. Choose the combination you will call forward. Set PWM to 0, 64, 128, 180, and 255. Speed should rise. Write down the triple (IN1, IN2, PWM) that Chapter 07 will treat as positive.
4. With PWM back at a modest value, swap the two motor wires once. Watch the shaft reverse. Restore the wires. Write one sentence: a swapped right-hand plug will reverse the sign of yaw.

### Expected results

`lab-notes.md` contains a four-row table with columns IN1, IN2, PWM, and observed behavior, plus the forward triple and the 5.22 V calculation for a 180/255 command on 7.4 V if that was your supply. If you used another voltage, recompute $$D \cdot V_s$$ for the supply you measured.

### Faults

| What you see | What to check |
|--------------|----------------|
| Shaft dead, driver hot | A shoot-through state or a shorted lead. PWM to 0 and remove VM. |
| Shaft dead, driver cool | Enable or STBY held inactive, VM missing, or the PWM GPIO is not the pin you think. |
| Board resets when the motor starts | Motor current is coming through USB. Move VM to the battery and keep grounds common. |
| Direction opposite your notes | Motor plug swapped, or IN1 and IN2 mirrored relative to the table. |
| Much slower than the duty suggests | Bridge drop (especially an L298N) or a battery sagging under load. |

## Exercises

1. A motor on a 7.4 V pack has winding resistance $$5~\Omega$$. Estimate the stall current.
2. PWM is $$100/255$$ and $$V_s = 6.0~\mathrm{V}$$. Compute $$V_{avg}$$ before the bridge drop. If the bridge then drops 1.8 V, what is left for the motor?
3. In words, which two switches are on for forward, and which two are on for reverse?
4. Your table says IN1 = 1, IN2 = 0, PWM > 0 is forward. The right-hand motor plug is reversed on the finished robot. What does a positive `angular.z` do?
5. Why do you write your own coast-versus-brake row instead of copying a classmate's L298N table onto a TB6612?

### Answer guidance

Stall current is $$7.4 / 5 \approx 1.48~\mathrm{A}$$. Duty $$100/255 \approx 0.392$$ gives about $$2.35~\mathrm{V}$$ before drop, and about $$0.55~\mathrm{V}$$ after 1.8 V, so the shaft may not turn. Forward is high-side left with low-side right; reverse is high-side right with low-side left. A reversed right plug makes positive yaw turn the wrong way. Coast and brake are properties of that chip's truth table, so a table from a different driver will lie about the stopped state.
