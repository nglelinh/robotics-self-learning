Estimated time: **~90 minutes**.

## Learning objectives

You will write a discrete PID update for one wheel’s speed, using encoder counts as the measurement, and predict the sign of each term on a numerical step. You will tune in the order P, then D, then a little I, with the wheel in the air, and you will clamp the integrator so a stalled wheel cannot wind up. You will log setpoint, measured speed, and output for a single step and say which term is misbehaving if the plot oscillates or never quite arrives.

## Prerequisites

Chapter 04’s encoder counts are trustworthy: you can convert a difference of counts into a speed. Chapter 05’s driver accepts a signed PWM or a signed −100…100 command. Lesson 08-05’s fixed period is the right home for this code; if you have not measured the loop yet, still call the PID on a known $$\Delta t$$ rather than “whenever `loop()` happens to return.” Calculus you need is only “rate of change” and “accumulated area.”

## Why this sits on the path

Open-loop PWM from Chapter 07 is enough to teleop on a smooth floor and a fresh battery. It is not enough once the battery sags, one wheel sees carpet, or Nav2 asks for 0.20 m/s and actually gets 0.11 m/s. Odometry in Chapter 11 integrates that error into a bent map. A learned policy in Chapter 12 that outputs wheel velocities assumes the wheels approximately do what they were told. PID on wheel speed is the small loop underneath `cmd_vel`. Do not jump to balancing a two-wheel robot; get one wheel to hold a speed first.

## Concepts

The error is setpoint minus measurement, in the same units on both sides. If the setpoint is 0.30 m/s of wheel tread and the encoder says 0.20 m/s, $$e = 0.10$$ m/s. Positive error means “go faster forward.” If you flip the sign, the controller runs away. Check the sign before you touch the gains.

$$
e(t) = r(t) - y(t)
$$

$$
u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^{t} e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d \frac{de}{dt}
$$

$$u$$ is the driver command, not a voltage you invent. If the driver wants −100…100, $$u$$ lives on that scale and must be saturated there. The three gains have units. $$K_p$$ is “command per (m/s).” A dimensionless $$K_p$$ copied from a tutorial about degrees is meaningless on this robot.

On a microcontroller the integral and derivative are sums and differences over the last period:

$$
I_k = \mathrm{clamp}\big(I_{k-1} + e_k \Delta t,\; -I_{\max},\; I_{\max}\big)
$$

$$
u_k = \mathrm{sat}\Big(K_p e_k + K_i I_k + K_d \frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{\Delta t}\Big)
$$

$$\mathrm{sat}$$ clamps $$u$$ to the driver range. The clamp on $$I$$ is anti-windup. Without it, a wheel blocked by a hand for two seconds stores a huge integral. When you let go, $$u$$ slams to the rail. Clamp $$I$$, and also freeze $$I$$ while $$u$$ is already saturated and the error still has the same sign. That second rule is “conditional integration.” Either rule is enough for this lab; implement one and write its name in the log.

Derivative of the error kicks when the setpoint steps, because $$e$$ jumps even if the wheel has not moved. A calmer variant differentiates the measurement instead, with a minus sign, so a setpoint change does not spike $$u$$. For a first tune, a small $$K_d$$ on the error is fine if the step is modest.

Tuning order on a wheel in the air:

1. $$K_i = 0$$, $$K_d = 0$$. Raise $$K_p$$ until a step from 0 to 0.3 m/s rises quickly and shows a small overshoot or a steady buzz. Back off a little.
2. Add $$K_d$$ until the overshoot shrinks and the buzz does not get louder. Encoder noise loves a large $$K_d$$. If the PWM hisses at rest, $$K_d$$ is too high or the speed estimate is too raw. Average two or three count windows before you add more D.
3. Add a little $$K_i$$ until the steady error (battery droop, friction) disappears over a second or two. If the wheel slowly oscillates with a period near a second, cut $$K_i$$ in half.

Ziegler–Nichols is a textbook recipe that expects you to bring the loop to the edge of instability. Skip it on a geared motor tied to a robot chassis. The manual order above is the lab.

## Worked example

One sample, not a whole trace. $$\Delta t = 0.02$$ s (50 Hz). Setpoint $$r = 0.30$$ m/s. Measured $$y_k = 0.18$$ m/s, so $$e_k = 0.12$$. Previous error $$e_{k-1} = 0.20$$. Integrator coming in: $$I_{k-1} = 0.04$$. Gains: $$K_p = 80$$, $$K_i = 40$$, $$K_d = 0.5$$, command saturated at ±100. $$I_{\max} = 0.5$$.

$$
I_k = 0.04 + 0.12 \times 0.02 = 0.0424
$$

$$
P = 80 \times 0.12 = 9.6
$$

$$
I_{\text{term}} = 40 \times 0.0424 = 1.696
$$

$$
D = 0.5 \times \frac{0.12 - 0.20}{0.02} = 0.5 \times (-4) = -2.0
$$

$$
u = 9.6 + 1.696 - 2.0 = 9.3
$$

The derivative is negative because the error got smaller: the wheel is already catching the setpoint, so D eases off the command. If your logged D is positive while the error is shrinking, the difference is backwards. Integer PWM code should round 9.3 to 9 and still log the float.

A second mental check: measurement above the setpoint, $$e < 0$$, must produce a smaller (or negative) $$u$$ if the wheel can reverse. If positive error and negative error both increase PWM, the measurement sign is flipped relative to the driver’s forward direction. Fix that before any gain change. Swapping two motor wires and also negating the encoder is a classic double negative that hides until the robot curves.

## Lab

Wheels off the ground. Battery connected as in Chapter 07. Driver logic supply and motor supply both present, common ground with the MCU.

1. Print `count` at 10 Hz and rotate the wheel by hand one revolution. Confirm the count sign matches the sign you will call “forward.”
2. Convert speed: $$y = \Delta \mathrm{counts} \times \frac{2\pi r}{N \Delta t}$$ with $$r$$ the wheel radius in metres and $$N$$ the counts per revolution. Log $$y$$ while you spin by hand. A slow hand spin should be a few tenths of a metre per second, not 40.
3. Implement the update with $$K_i = K_d = 0$$ and a step to 0.25 m/s for 2 seconds, then 0.
4. Record CSV: `t_ms,r,y,e,P,I,D,u`.
5. Add D, then I, using the order above. Stop if the gearbox chatters.

**Expected shape of the log**

```text
t_ms,r,y,u
0,0.25,0.00,20
200,0.25,0.16,14
800,0.25,0.24,8
2000,0.00,0.22,-15
2400,0.00,0.02,0
```

Numbers will not match yours. The shape must: $$u$$ positive while $$y$$ is below $$r$$, $$y$$ near $$r$$ before the step ends, $$u$$ opposite when the setpoint drops, and $$u$$ near 0 at rest. After you stall the wheel with a finger for one second (gently, in the air), releasing it must not produce a full-scale twitch. If it does, $$I$$ is not clamped.

**Failure modes**

| What you see | Likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Wheel runs away as soon as PID enables | Error sign, or encoder direction opposite the motor |
| Steady buzz at the target | $$K_p$$ or $$K_d$$ too high, or $$\Delta t$$ jitter from `Serial.print` every loop |
| Never reaches the setpoint, $$u$$ stuck at 40 | No integral and the P term is saturated by a low gain against friction. On a free wheel, friction is small; if you need huge $$u$$ in the air, the speed unit is wrong |
| Fine in the air, crawls on carpet | That is load, not a broken PID. Raise I slightly or accept a lower max speed. Do not copy the in-air $$K_p$$ onto the floor without a new log |
| Integral explodes while you hold the wheel | $$I_{\max}$$ missing |

## Exercises

1. Recompute the worked example with $$e_k = -0.05$$ and $$e_{k-1} = 0.12$$. What is the sign of $$u$$ if $$I$$ is still small? Guidance: P is negative, D is also negative because the error fell through zero toward more negative, so $$u$$ should command a slowdown or a reverse.
2. Your CSV shows $$y$$ stuck at 0 while $$u$$ climbs to 100 and stays there. Which clamp failed? Guidance: the integrator. P alone would sit at $$K_p e$$, which is constant if $$y$$ is stuck.
3. Why is wheel-speed PID the wrong first tool for “drive exactly 1.00 m down the hallway”? Guidance: that is an outer position loop. Speed PID only makes the tread fast. Distance needs an integral of speed, and heading needs both wheels.
4. A blog uses $$K_p = 2$$ for a heater in degrees. What experiment replaces copying that number? Guidance: the in-air step, raising $$K_p$$ from a small value until the rise looks brisk.
5. Write the one-line contract between this lesson and `cmd_vel`: who outputs m/s, who outputs PWM? Guidance: kinematics (08-04) outputs two wheel speeds in m/s; this PID outputs PWM; the bridge must not skip the middle.
